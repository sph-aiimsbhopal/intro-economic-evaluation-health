The two charts are the same data. Drag λ and watch the dashed line sweep the cloud while the dot walks the curve: the height of the curve at any λ is simply the share of the cloud that falls below the line there. That is all a cost-effectiveness acceptability curve is, and it is counted on net monetary benefit (λ·ΔE − ΔC > 0), not on the ratio, because negative and infinite ICERs make counting on the ratio incoherent.

What it does not tell you. Not the probability that the drug works, only that it is good value given the model. It captures parameter uncertainty and nothing else: if the model's structure is wrong, every one of these simulations is wrong together. A curve hovering near 50% across all plausible thresholds is an argument for more research, not a coin toss.

Parameters here are illustrative, chosen to show the mechanism. For a real Indian example, read Gupta N, Verma RK, Gupta S, Prinja S. Cost effectiveness of trastuzumab for management of breast cancer in India. JCO Glob Oncol 2020;6:205–16 (PMID 32045547) — a 4% probability of being cost-effective on one effectiveness source and 57.3% on another, rising to 90% with a 15–35% price cut. Same drug, same country, same year.
