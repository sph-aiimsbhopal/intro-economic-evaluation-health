Making decisions
in health care

Drummond et al., Methods for the Economic Evaluation of Health Care Programmes, 4th ed., 2015.
Chapter 2, pages 19 to 41.

Faculty, residents and research project staff  ·  AIIMS Bhopal, 23 September 2026

Sessions 2 to 5 built an ICER and then ranked options with it. This session is about what that number is worth. Say plainly at the start: an ICER on its own is inert. It becomes a decision only when you set it against something, and this session is about where that something comes from, and how contested it is.

By the end of this session

  1. Explain the budget constraint and health opportunity cost
  2. Tell a demand-side threshold from a supply-side one
  3. State India's willingness to pay per QALY, and say what it is not
  4. Say why the 1 to 3 times GDP rule was abandoned by its own authors
  5. Describe how HTAIn turns a topic into a recommendation
  6. Give two reasons a cost-effective intervention might still not be funded
Objective 2 is the intellectual core of Chapter 2 and the one they find genuinely surprising: two respectable methods give different thresholds, and the difference decides whether a programme is funded.

The number we borrowed

In the last session you ranked strategies against λ ≈ ₹2,12,307 per QALY, and took that number on trust.

Where does it come from, and should you believe it?

The same gap sits under this morning's ₹54,881 per QALY for VIA screening. Is that good value? Nobody in this room could say, and neither could the literature, in India, until very recently.

ICER from Chauhan, Prinja et al., PLoS One, 2020. Threshold from Chugh et al., Value in Health, 2026.

Take a show of hands on the ₹54,881: good value, bad value, do not know. Then point out that "do not know" was the only defensible answer all morning, because the question was incomplete. A ratio needs something on the other side of the inequality, and this session is where that something comes from. They have now earned the question: they built an ICER in Session 5 and borrowed a threshold to use it.

The threshold, λ

The most you are willing to pay for one additional unit of health.

Adopt if ICER < λ. Reject if ICER > λ. Arithmetically trivial.

The whole difficulty of this chapter is: where does λ come from?

There are two quite different ways of arriving at it. They give different numbers, and the profession has not settled which is right.

Resist giving them a number yet. If you give a number first they will memorise it and stop thinking. Make them sit with the question for two slides.

Two answers, from opposite directions

Demand side: willingness to pay

Ask society what a unit of health is worth to it. Survey people, or infer it from what they pay for safety elsewhere.

Answers: what should we be prepared to spend?

Supply side: health opportunity cost

Ask what health the system actually produces at the margin. With a fixed budget, spending here displaces care there.

Answers: what can we afford without doing net harm?

With a fixed budget, only the supply-side number keeps you from doing net harm.

Chapter 2's central argument, and Claxton's life's work. Make it concrete: if you adopt something at ₹3 lakh per QALY while the marginal rupee in the NHM buys a QALY for ₹80,000, you have not bought health. You have traded away more than you gained. The programme looks like a success and the population is worse off.

The rule you were probably taught

Cost per DALY averted below 1× GDP per capita is "highly cost-effective". Below 3× is "cost-effective".

From the Commission on Macroeconomics and Health and the WHO-CHOICE programme. It spread because it was simple, universal, and needed no country data.

WHO's own authors have argued it should be retired.

It is a demand-side heuristic with no link to any actual health budget. Applying it approves interventions a country cannot fund without displacing more health than it creates.

Bertram et al., Bulletin of the WHO, 2016. Marseille et al., Bulletin of the WHO, 2015.

Many in the room will have used this rule, in a paper of their own or in work they supervised. Say so without embarrassment: it was standard practice. The point is that the field moved. A paper concluding "cost-effective at 3× GDP per capita" now invites a hard question from a reviewer.

Their conclusion: routinely used thresholds have been too high.

Every upper bound sits below 59% of GDP per capita, well under the 1× that the old rule treated as "highly cost-effective".

Woods, Revill, Sculpher, Claxton, Value in Health, 2016.

That gap between 59% and 300% is the single most consequential unresolved number in global health economics. The authors themselves present these as a first attempt requiring further research, which is worth teaching as well.

India now has its own estimate

₹2,12,307mean willingness to pay per QALY (US$2,535)
1.0 to 1.52×GDP per capita, depending on the health state
5,460respondents in 6 states, 21,640 observations

Multistage stratified random sample. Twelve hypothetical health states, time trade-off for the health gain and willingness to pay for its value, discounted at 3%. Across health states the estimate ranged from ₹1,70,414 to ₹2,58,985.

Chugh, Jyani, Kar, Rajsekar, Prinja et al., Value in Health, 2026.

The first Indian estimate, from a large PGIMER-led national study with Department of Health Research involvement. Note who is on the author list: the same group wrote the Indian Reference Case, so this is likely to shape HTAIn practice.

Which fits the decision taken in February 2026. But notice the dates: the analysis was published in 2020 and the threshold estimate in 2026. Policy did not wait for a number.

ICERs from Chauhan et al., 2020. Threshold from Chugh et al., 2026.

The satisfying moment of the workshop: the question parked since Session 2 resolves, and the threshold borrowed in Session 5 acquires a source. Then immediately complicate it. Decisions get made on the evidence available, not the evidence you wish existed.

One caveat you must carry

₹2,12,307 is a demand-side number: what a sample of Indians said a QALY is worth to them.

It is not an estimate of what the Indian health system gives up at the margin when it spends an extra rupee.

If the supply-side threshold is lower, judging by willingness to pay will approve things the budget cannot absorb.

That is not a criticism of the study, which is careful and says what it measured. It is the open question the field is living with. Teach it as open.

If anyone wants one number to carry away: use ₹2,12,307 as the current reference, cite Chugh 2026, and state the demand-side caveat in one sentence. That is defensible in a committee or a review, and it is honest.

League tables

Rank every intervention by cost per QALY, draw a line where the budget runs out, fund everything above it. India now has one: 79 studies, 2003 to 2019.

Then read the fine print of that same review.

70%reported DALYs, not QALYs
23%did not discount at all
73%did any sensitivity analysis
18%reported budget impact

It also needs a comparable evaluation of every competing use of the budget. Nobody has that. What was never studied never appears in the table.

Shah, Singh, Kotwani et al., Frontiers in Public Health, 2022.

This pays off the QALY versus DALY warning from Sessions 2 and 5. Seventy per cent of the table is in one currency of health and thirty per cent in another, and a quarter of the entries are undiscounted. You cannot rank those against each other honestly. Press the completeness point too: the missing rows are the real problem, and they are missing for reasons that correlate with who has research funding.

A country that publishes its threshold

NICE has used an explicit range since 1999. It is the clearest example anywhere of a threshold you can argue with.

Until March 2026From April 2026
Standard range£20,000 to £30,000 per QALY£25,000 to £35,000 per QALY

Announced 1 December 2025. NICE expects it to allow roughly 3 to 5 additional medicines a year, from a base where about 91% of appraised medicines are already recommended.

NICE, Changes to NICE's cost-effectiveness thresholds confirmed, 1 December 2025.

Stress that the mere existence of a published number is the achievement. It can be scrutinised, modelled, and, as here, changed in public. India's equivalent is still implicit.

Why did it move?

Not because anyone re-estimated health opportunity cost in the NHS.

The stated purpose was to unlock innovation and support the sector, improving the UK's operating environment for the life sciences industry.

A threshold raised for industrial policy is no longer measuring what the theory says it measures.

Health economists have said so in print: such changes risk making cost-effectiveness analysis performative rather than informative.

Vallejo-Torres, Edney, Espinosa, Karnon, Longo, Applied Health Economics and Health Policy, 2026.

Discussion slide, three or four minutes. Ask: if raising the threshold means more medicines are approved from a fixed NHS budget, who pays? Patients in services that get squeezed, who are invisible because no committee ever names them. That invisibility is what health opportunity cost is about, and it is why this belongs in Chapter 2 and not in a news round-up.

Who decides in India

Health Technology Assessment in India (HTAIn), now an attached office of the Department of Health Research, Ministry of Health and Family Welfare.

Board

Highest decision-making body. Policy-makers, clinicians and government experts. Endorses recommendations.

Technical Appraisal Committee

Economists, clinicians, researchers, social scientists. Quality assurance and technical stewardship.

Secretariat

In-house scientists and analysts. Prioritises topics, coordinates, monitors progress.

Regional Resource Centres

Government institutes across the country that conduct the studies and build capacity in the states. The network keeps growing.

Several of the Regional Resource Centres sit in departments like theirs. Say that out loud. This is a career route, not an abstraction: PGIMER, JIPMER, AIIMS Bhubaneswar and the IIPHs all appear on the author lists we have been citing all morning.

From a question to a policy

  1. A user department, a programme or a state, submits a priority topic
  2. The Secretariat prioritises it and takes it to the Technical Appraisal Committee
  3. The Committee allocates it to a Regional Resource Centre, which writes a proposal
  4. The study runs. The report goes back to the Committee for approval
  5. The Board gives final approval
  6. Recommendations return to the user department for implementation

Any of you can nominate a topic.

Two things to draw out. The loop closes back at the user department: HTAIn advises, it does not command, and implementation sits with the programme. And topic selection is where the real power lies. Whatever never gets nominated never gets evaluated.

Cost-effective is not the same as funded

ConsiderationThe question it asks
Budget impactCost-effective per patient, but can we afford it for everyone at once?
EquityWho gains and who is left out? Does it widen or narrow a gap?
FeasibilityCold chain, trained staff, supply, referral pathways. Do they exist?
AcceptabilityWill families consent? Will clinicians adopt it?
TimingDo we act on what we have, or wait for better evidence?

The economic evaluation is one input to a decision, never the decision.

Extended cost-effectiveness analysis brings equity into the arithmetic. See Dixit, Prinja, Jyani et al., Lancet Global Health, 2023.

Drummond makes this point at the end of Chapter 2 and it is easy to skip. Do not. Anyone who believes the ICER decides becomes insufferable in committees and is ignored. Anyone who can say "this is cost-effective, here is the budget impact, and here is who gets left out" are listened to.

A live case worth arguing about

Cost per DALY averted, healthcare perspective, PRIME model, 12-year-old girls in India:

Vaccine (types covered)Incremental cost per DALY averted
Nonavalent, Gardasil 9 (6, 11, 16, 18 + 31, 33, 45, 52, 58)US$ 362.78
Quadrivalent, Gardasil 4 (6, 11, 16, 18)US$ 393.16
Bivalent, Cervarix (16, 18)US$ 432.24

Assuming a constant price per dose, the nonavalent vaccine dominated both others.

India launched in February 2026 with a single dose of the quadrivalent.

Arora and Tuffaha, Asia-Pacific Journal of Clinical Oncology, 2024.

Open the floor before turning the page. Get them to say the word "price" out loud. Nine types for the price of four is exactly the assumption no procurement officer would accept.

So why not the nonavalent?

Price is not constant

The dominance result assumes every product costs the same per dose. Gardasil 9 does not sell at the Gardasil 4 price. Change that one input and the ranking changes.

16 and 18 do most of the damage

About 83% of invasive cervical cancer in India is attributable to HPV 16 or 18. The five extra types buy less than the count suggests.

Financing

A US$ 250 million GAVI partnership, and a negotiated price, attach to a particular product rather than to a valency.

Supply at national scale

About 1.15 crore girls a year. Guaranteed volume beats a marginally better ICER that cannot be delivered.

Take these two slides together. Start with price, because it is the assumption the paper itself flags, and let them work out that nine types at the four-type price is not a market anyone has seen.

Three more that mattered

Domestic manufacturing

Cervavac, from the Serum Institute of India, is also quadrivalent. It was still seeking WHO prequalification at launch, a procurement gate rather than a question of value.

Budget impact

Whether it is affordable this year is a separate test from whether it is cost-effective.

Delivery

Cold chain, school and session logistics, and a single-dose schedule shape what is actually achievable.

Every one of these is legitimate, and none of them is in the ICER.

Land the block here. The ICER was not ignored and it was not wrong. It answered the question it was asked, under an assumption that did not hold, and then these other considerations decided the case. That is what "one input to a decision" means in practice. If someone argues the nonavalent should still have been chosen, that is a good argument to have, and the way to have it is to re-run the analysis at real prices rather than to assert it.

The threshold, made visible

The origin is current practice. Drag the intervention between quadrants, then drag λ and watch the same point flip from adopt to reject with no number in the analysis changing.

Preset to ₹2,12,307 per QALY  ·  sph-aiimsbhopal.github.io/intro-economic-evaluation-health

Five minutes. They met this widget in Session 5, when they were ranking strategies; now λ itself is the thing that moves. Two things to have them try. Park an intervention just below the line, then drag λ down to a plausible supply-side value and watch it become unaffordable. That is the Woods argument made physical. Then the south-west quadrant, where the decision rule reverses. Disinvestment lives there, and almost nobody looks for it.

Recap

  • λ turns an ICER into a decision. Everything hard here is where λ comes from
  • Demand-side and supply-side give different numbers. Only the second respects a fixed budget
  • India's first estimate is ₹2,12,307 per QALY. Demand-side, so carry the caveat
  • League tables still mix QALYs and DALYs, so they cannot be read straight down
  • HTAIn advises. User departments decide.
If you are running late, the two lines that must survive are the demand versus supply distinction, and "cost-effective is not the same as funded".

Where the numbers
came from

Every ICER today arrived already calculated, over a lifetime nobody observed. The next session is the machinery that produces them: decision trees and Markov models.

Before the next session: find the figure in your group's paper that shows its model structure. Most papers have one.

Set that pre-read explicitly, it makes the modelling session concrete rather than abstract. If a group's paper has no model diagram, that is itself worth reporting back.
