Economic Evaluation in Health  ·  Faculty workshop  ·  Session 2

What economic evaluation is,
and what it is not

Drummond et al., Methods for the Economic Evaluation of Health Care Programmes, 4th ed., 2015.
Chapter 1, pages 1 to 19.

Faculty, residents and research project staff  ·  AIIMS Bhopal, 23 September 2026

Open by saying what this block is not. It is not about saving money, and it is not accounting. Set the expectation that in fifty minutes they will be able to pick up any paper with the word "cost" in the title and say precisely what kind of study it is, and whether it can answer an allocation question at all. That is a concrete, testable skill.

By the end of this block

  1. Define economic evaluation and state the two features that make an analysis one
  2. Explain opportunity cost in your own words
  3. Use Drummond's two questions to sort any study into one of six boxes
  4. Tell CMA, CEA, CUA and CBA apart
  5. Say why a costing study is not a cost-effectiveness analysis
Read these out. Objective 5 is the one most often got wrong, in print as much as in supervision, so flag it now and again at the end.

Labour this slide. The commonest flaw in the papers they will read is a bad comparator, usually "do nothing" when the real alternative was something else. We return to it hard in Block 3. For now, plant it: an evaluation is only as good as the alternatives it compared.

Start here: resources are scarce

A District Health Society has a fixed envelope this year. Every rupee committed to one thing is a rupee not available for another.

This is not a budgeting inconvenience. It is the reason economic evaluation exists.

Scarcity is not a failure of planning. Even a very rich health system faces it, because demand for health care grows faster than any budget.

If someone objects that the government can print money, the constraint is real resources: ANMs, cold-chain capacity, operation theatre hours, oncologists. Money is only the accounting unit for those things.

Opportunity cost

The value of the benefit forgone because a resource was not used in its next best alternative way.

The cost of the HPV programme is not ₹X crore.

The cost is the health that ₹X crore would have produced somewhere else in the Indian health system.

Money is the measuring rod. Forgone health is the thing being measured.

The single most important idea in the course, and the one most likely to be recited without being understood. Test it: ask what the opportunity cost of a new machine in their department is. The post, the drug budget or the ward it displaces, and the health that would have produced. Force them to name the alternative. An opportunity cost with no named alternative is not one.

So the question is never…

Is this intervention worth it?

Is it worth it compared with what we would otherwise do with the same resources?

Which is why an economic evaluation always has at least two arms. And why "cost-effective" is an empty phrase unless you can finish the sentence: cost-effective compared with what, at what threshold, from whose point of view?

Get them to say the full sentence out loud once. Cost-effective compared with what, at what threshold, from whose perspective. That sentence is worth more to them than any formula here, and it is the fastest way to interrogate a drug representative's slide deck.

Two features define an economic evaluation

1. Inputs and outputs

It deals with both the resources consumed (costs) and the results produced (consequences). Not one or the other.

2. Choice

It compares two or more alternatives. No comparison, no choice. No choice, no economics.

Miss either feature and you have something else. Often something useful, but not an economic evaluation.

Everything in the next ten minutes is just these two features turned into a table. Tell them that, so the grid does not feel arbitrary.

Drummond's two questions

Question 1

Are both costs and consequences examined?

Question 2

Is there a comparison of two or more alternatives?

Two yes-or-no questions. Six possible boxes. Only one of them is a full economic evaluation.

Ask them to write these two questions inside the cover of whatever they use for notes. They will use them for the rest of their careers. Six boxes and not four, because "costs only" and "consequences only" are separate answers to Question 1.

The spine of the block. Same disease, same country, six different questions. Emphasise Sankaranarayanan: a landmark Indian trial in 130,000 women showing that a single round of HPV testing cut cervical cancer deaths, and still a partial evaluation in Drummond's scheme because it costed nothing. Being partial is not a criticism. It is a statement about which question a study can answer.

Guard against the impression that partial studies are second rate. Most departmental work, and most theses they supervise, will be partial studies, and that is fine as long as they are labelled honestly. The sin is calling a cost analysis a cost-effectiveness analysis.

Inside the green box: four types

In every full economic evaluation, costs are measured in money.

What differs is how the consequences are valued.

That one distinction gives you CMA, CEA, CUA and CBA.

If you remember nothing else about the four types, remember that the difference lives entirely on the consequences side.

It is easy to hold four definitions and still not tell the types apart on a real paper. Give the organising principle instead: look at what is in the denominator.

Cost-minimisation analysis

Consequences valued as: nothing. They are shown to be equivalent, then set aside.
The output: which option costs less.

Published example. Ready-made versus custom-made spectacles for school children in India. A non-inferiority trial first established that spectacle wear was equivalent, so the economic analysis reduced to a cost comparison. Cost per child needing spectacles fell from US$26.91 to US$11.15, a saving of 58.6%.

Only legitimate if equivalence was demonstrated, not assumed.

Minakaran et al., Ophthalmic Epidemiology, 2021.

A textbook-clean CMA, worth dwelling on because CMA has fallen out of favour. Most agencies now discourage it, since "no statistically significant difference" is not the same as "equivalent". Note that this study earned its CMA: a non-inferiority trial came first. Most papers claiming CMA have not done that.

Cost-effectiveness analysis

Consequences valued as: one natural unit. Deaths averted, cases detected, life-years gained.
The output: cost per unit of effect.

Published example. A national rotavirus vaccination programme for India. At US$1.00 per dose it would cost US$49.8 million and prevent about 44,000 deaths a year, at US$662.94 per life saved. At the GAVI price of $0.15 a dose it was cost-saving.

You can only compare programmes that share the same outcome unit.

Esposito et al., Clinical Infectious Diseases, 2011.

Note the vaccine price doing all the work: cost-saving at $0.15, still highly cost-effective at $7.00. Price is almost always the most influential parameter in a vaccine evaluation, which previews one-way sensitivity analysis in Block 3.

Cost-utility analysis

Consequences valued as: a generic, preference-weighted measure. The QALY, or in much LMIC work the DALY.
The output: cost per QALY gained.

Published example. A Markov model from a societal perspective over a lifetime, comparing screening strategies and HPV vaccination for India. Screening with VIA every 5 years: ₹54,881 per QALY gained. HPV vaccination, alone or with later screening: ₹5,693 to ₹31,511 per QALY gained.

A generic unit makes a cancer programme and a cardiac programme comparable.

Chauhan, Prinja et al., PLoS One, 2020.

This is the paper that most directly informs the February 2026 decision, and it is Indian, PGIMER-led, societal perspective, with primary Indian cost and quality-of-life data. Point at those ICERs and ask what they should be compared against. Nobody can answer yet, which is exactly the cliff-hanger into Block 2.

Cost-benefit analysis

Consequences valued as: money, through willingness to pay or lost production.
The output: net benefit in rupees, or a benefit-to-cost ratio.

Why you will read very few of these in health. Valuing a life-year in rupees is technically demanding and ethically contested, and most health ministries do not want their decisions framed that way. CBA remains standard in transport, environment and infrastructure.

Its unique strength: it can compare a hospital with a road.

Be honest that CBA is rare in health and do not oversell it. But make the theoretical point: CEA and CUA can only rank options within a fixed health budget, and only CBA can question the size of that budget. That is a real limitation of everything else we teach.

A warning: QALYs are not DALYs

QALY, gained

Health gained. Preference-based utility weights, 1 for full health and 0 for dead, usually elicited from the general public. You maximise it.

DALY, averted

Health lost. Disability weights plus years of life lost against a reference life expectancy. You minimise it.

They are not interchangeable, and one is not the arithmetic negative of the other.

You met DALYs first, through burden-of-disease teaching. Most Indian HTA work now reports QALYs, because the Indian Reference Case asks for them. Watch which one a paper uses.

This slide exists because they will otherwise conflate the two for the rest of their careers. Concretely: Arora 2024 reports cost per DALY averted, Chauhan 2020 reports cost per QALY gained. Same disease, same country, different currency of health. Those two numbers cannot go in the same league table.

Three errors we will keep catching

  1. Calling a costing study a CEA. "Cost" in the title does not make it an economic evaluation. Ask Question 1.
  2. Calling a CEA a CUA. Cost per case detected is not cost per QALY. Look at the denominator.
  3. Declaring something cost-effective with no threshold and no comparator. The phrase is empty without both.

A systematic review of Indian evaluations found exactly these problems at scale.

Prinja et al., Applied Health Economics and Health Policy, 2015.

Tell them plainly: they will meet all three in journal club within the year, and probably in a pharmaceutical presentation within the month. Naming the error politely and precisely is a professional skill.

Why this matters in your job

State PIP

Every activity proposed displaces another. Somebody has to justify the choice.

PMJAY package rates

What a procedure is reimbursed at is a costing and value question, revised using these methods.

HTAIn topic nomination

Any of you can nominate a technology for assessment. Knowing what evidence is needed is the first step.

Work you supervise

Label the study honestly. A good cost analysis beats a mislabelled CEA in any thesis or paper.

Make it concrete. Ask who has sat on a purchase or a drug and therapeutics committee, or worked on a State PIP. Get them to describe a choice they saw made and what evidence, if any, supported it.

Recap

  • Opportunity cost, the health forgone elsewhere, is why any of this exists
  • An economic evaluation examines both costs and consequences, for two or more alternatives
  • The two questions sort every study into six boxes. Five are partial
  • The four full types differ only in how consequences are valued

Nothing so far tells you whether ₹54,881 per QALY is good value.

End on the open question deliberately. It is the hook into Block 2, and it is a genuinely unsettled question in India rather than a teaching device.
