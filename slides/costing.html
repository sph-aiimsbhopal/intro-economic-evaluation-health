Prices are not costs

In 2018, many PM-JAY package prices bore little relation to what the care cost to deliver.

Share of packages priced below half their measured costShare
2018, before revision~42%
2019, after costing evidence was used to revise them~20%

In the revision, 61% of package prices went up and 18% came down. The evidence was hospital costing across public tertiary hospitals.

A price is what someone pays. A cost is what the care uses up.

Prinja, Singh, Rajsekar et al., Applied Health Economics and Health Policy, 2021.

The hook. Costing is not an accountant's hobby; it moved national reimbursement rates. Ask who in the room has ever been told what a procedure "costs" and later found that figure was a charge, a tender price or a package rate. All three are prices.

Three steps, every time

Identify

Which resources does the care use? Staff, drugs, consumables, tests, equipment, space, the share of the hospital that keeps the lights on.

Measure

How much of each, per episode? Minutes, units, days, uses.

Value

What is each unit worth? Salary per minute, purchase price, annuitised capital, allocated overhead.

Most errors happen at identify: something is simply never counted.

Session 3 named these three steps while framing the question. Here we practise them on a service in this room, which is where they stop being abstract. The commonest omissions: capital, shared overheads, and the time of staff who are "already paid for".

"But the nurse is already paid for"

An economic cost is the value of what the resource could otherwise have done.

ResourceFinancial cost to the budgetEconomic cost
Staff time on the new servicezero extra, if salariedthe minutes, at salary per minute
Donated equipmentzeroits market value, annuitised
A room that already existszeroits share of the building

Economic evaluation uses economic cost, because the question is what else those minutes could have done.

This is opportunity cost from Session 2 arriving at ward level. The nurse spending 45 minutes on the new procedure is not spending them somewhere else. That is the cost.

Two choices about method

Bottom up: count what each patient uses
Top down: divide a total by the output
Micro
costing:
every item
Most precise. Minutes timed, items counted, priced one by one. Slow; can miss shared costs.
Department totals split finely by activity. Captures overheads; needs good accounts.
Gross
costing:
big blocks
A few large components per patient: a bed-day, an operation, a course of drugs.
Fastest. Annual budget ÷ patients. Every patient looks average.

Precision where the money is; gross costing for the rest.

Two separate questions: how finely do you cut (micro versus gross), and which direction do you come from (bottom up versus top down). People conflate them. Most real studies mix all four cells, micro-costing the expensive, variable items and gross-costing the small ones.

Bottom up and top down, side by side

Bottom up

Follow patients. Time the staff, count the items, price each.

Sees variation between patients.
Misses anything nobody thought to count: supervision, idle time, overheads.

Top down

Start from the department's annual spend. Divide by patients, or bed-days.

Sees every rupee the department spends.
Misses that a complex case uses more than a simple one.

India's national hospital costing uses both.

The Cost of Health Services in India study planned a mixed top-down and bottom-up method across 52 public and 40 private hospitals in 13 states.

Prinja, Singh, Guinness, Rajsekar, Bhargava, BMJ Open, 2020.

Bottom-up totals are usually lower than top-down totals for the same service, because bottom-up misses things. If a paper uses only bottom-up costing and reports a very low cost, ask what was left out.

Using all contracted hours rather than productive hours understates the cost per minute. The denominator is where people go wrong, not the numerator.

The annuity factor is the present value of one rupee a year for n years at 3%. Nobody needs the derivation today; they need to know that the table exists and that capital belongs in the cost.

Shared overheads: the costs nobody owns

Step down: every cost ends up at a centre that sees patients.

This is how the national hospital costing study allocates support and indirect service centres to patient care centres, using apportioning statistics. Keep it at this level. The point is that a ward's cost per bed-day includes a slice of the laundry and the administration, and a study that leaves them out is too cheap.

For the worksheet they will not know their hospital's laundry budget. That is fine: they should write down the allocation base they would use and a guess, and mark it as the number they are least sure of. That is question (b) on page 2.

Average cost is not the cost of one more

Adding ten scans a day to a machine that is idle half the time costs the consumables and the staff minutes. Not another machine.

Average cost

Total cost ÷ output. Right for pricing a service and for a programme at full scale.

Marginal cost

The extra cost of one more unit. Right for a small change at the edge of existing capacity.

Ask which one a paper used, and whether it fits the decision it is informing.

The reverse error matters too: a scale-up that needs a second machine has a marginal cost far above the average. Capacity constraints decide which is relevant.

If the connection allows, open the database for thirty seconds. Then point out that the Reference Case treats PM-JAY as the payer for curative care, so package rates are legitimately used as the cost to the payer in many HTAIn studies. That is a perspective choice, not an error, as long as it is labelled.

Five costing errors to catch in a paper

  1. Charges or prices used as costs, without saying so
  2. Capital left out, or charged in full to one year
  3. Overheads left out. Bottom-up alone, with no allocation
  4. Average cost used for a marginal decision, or the reverse
  5. Old costs, new decision: cost year and currency not stated, or not updated

Every one of them makes the intervention look cheaper, or hides that it is not.

These are the costing row of the appraisal checklist, principle 5, turned into things to look for. Tell the groups to check their paper for all five at lunch.

Your turn: the worksheet

5 min

Choose one episode your department delivers. Define where it starts and ends. Map the pathway.

20 min

The goal is not the right number. It is knowing which number you do not know.

Same groups as the paper exercise, which are formed by specialty cluster. Each group picks one member's department and costs an episode there. Walk round. The two most useful interventions: "what did you leave out?" and "which line would you measure properly first?" Take three groups' totals and their answer to question (b) in the last five minutes.

And your paper

  1. Where did its costs come from: a database, a trial, package rates, the literature?
  2. Bottom up, top down, or not stated?
  3. Whose costs: the hospital's, the payer's, the patient's?
  4. Which of the five errors can you find?

Write it in row 5 of the checklist before lunch.
