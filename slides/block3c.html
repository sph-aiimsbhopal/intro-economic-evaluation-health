Handling uncertainty

One-way and probabilistic sensitivity analysis, the tornado diagram, and the cost-effectiveness acceptability curve.

Drummond et al., 4th ed., and the Indian Reference Case, principles 9 to 12

The ICER inherits all of it, and reports none of it.

₹26,154 per QALY is the answer if every parameter sits at its central value at the same moment. That is one draw from a very large space.

The argument for the whole block. Point at any row and ask what happens to the ICER if that number is 20% different. They will not know, and that is the question the rest of the block answers.

Four things that get muddled

KindWhat it meansWhat you do
Parameter uncertaintywe do not know an input's true valueone-way and probabilistic analysis
Structural uncertaintythe model itself might be built wrongscenario analysis, alternative structures
Methodological uncertaintya defensible choice could have gone the other wayscenario analysis; follow the Reference Case
Heterogeneitythe true value genuinely differs between peoplesubgroup analysis, not sensitivity analysis

Probabilistic analysis handles the first of these and nothing else. Say so when you report it.

The heterogeneity row is the one they get wrong. Uncertainty is "we do not know". Heterogeneity is "we do know, and it varies". Averaging over real variation hides the possibility that an intervention is excellent for one group and useless for another.

9 Heterogeneity: decide subgroups first

An intervention may be cost-effective in one subgroup and nowhere else. That is a targeting opportunity, not a nuisance.

Identify subgroups at the planning stage, so stratified costs, effects and utilities are collected for each.

Picking subgroups after seeing the results is data dredging with an economics vocabulary. The Reference Case says so plainly. Justify the basis for each subgroup and have a clinician confirm it is sensible.

Heterogeneity and equity are, on published appraisals, among the weakest parts of Indian economic evaluations. Handling them well is a way to stand out.

A good example to offer: HPV vaccination in a cohort with high screening coverage versus one with almost none. Same vaccine, quite different value. Averaging the two gives a number that describes neither.

10 What India actually requires

Probabilistic sensitivity analysis

Recommended for all analyses. Not optional, not only for models, not only for large studies.

Acceptability curves

The recommended way to present the results of that analysis.

Deterministic sensitivity analysis

For key structural assumptions and for parameters likely to be influential.

Scenario analysis

Including an explicit best case and worst case.

Only 73% of the Indian evaluations reviewed had done any sensitivity analysis at all.

Sharma et al., 2023. Proportion from Shah et al., Frontiers in Public Health, 2022.

The word "all" is the one to stress. Anyone planning a costing study who intends to skip probabilistic analysis because "it is only a small study" is planning something the national standard does not accept.

One-way analysis: move one thing at a time

Take a parameter, swing it across its plausible range, hold everything else at its central value, and see what happens to the ICER.

Repeat for every parameter. Cheap, transparent, and it answers the question a decision-maker asks first:

Which number does this conclusion actually depend on?

The range must come from somewhere defensible: a published confidence interval, the spread across facilities in a costing study, the range of prices actually quoted. Not from a round number you liked.

Ranges chosen by convenience, "we varied everything by plus or minus 20%", are a real weakness in the Indian literature and easy to spot. Ask them to look for the justification of the range next time they read a sensitivity analysis.

The tornado diagram

One bar per parameter, showing the ICER at its low and high value. Sorted widest at the top, which is where the name comes from.

Widest bar

The parameter the answer turns on. Get this one right before anything else.

Bars crossing λ

Parameters that can flip the decision, not merely move the number.

Narrow bars

Precision here buys nothing. Stop arguing about them.

In the 2011 Indian rotavirus analysis the two parameters that mattered were vaccine price and efficacy against severe disease.

Esposito, Tate, Kang, Parashar, Clinical Infectious Diseases, 2011.

That finding is why the same vaccine was cost-saving at $0.15 a dose and merely cost-effective at $7.00. Price dominating a vaccine evaluation is close to a general rule, and it is why the negotiation matters more than the model.

Why one-way analysis is not enough

  • Parameters do not take turns. Several are wrong at once, and the joint effect is not the sum of the separate ones
  • Some are correlated. A facility with high staff costs usually has high overheads too
  • It cannot give you a probability. A tornado says the ICER could be anywhere from ₹1.05 lakh to ₹4.06 lakh, and nothing about how likely either end is

For a probability you must vary everything at once, thousands of times.

The third point motivates probabilistic analysis. Decision-makers ask how likely it is that this is good value, and one-way analysis simply cannot answer that, however many bars you draw.

Probabilistic sensitivity analysis

  1. Give every uncertain parameter a distribution, not a single value
  2. Draw one value from each: a complete, internally coherent set of inputs
  3. Run the model once with that set. Record ΔC and ΔE
  4. Repeat a thousand times, or ten thousand

The output is not a number. It is a distribution of possible answers.

Which is the honest representation, and the reason the Reference Case wants it for every analysis.

Emphasise step 2. One draw is a coherent set. You are not varying parameters independently in sequence, you are asking "what if the world were like this?" a thousand times.

Which distribution, and why

Parameter typeDistributionBecause
Probabilities, proportions, utilitiesBetabounded between 0 and 1, as they must be
Costs, lengths of stay, resource countsGamma or log-normalcannot go negative, and are right-skewed
Relative risks and odds ratioslog-normalsymmetric on the scale where they are estimated
A set of mutually exclusive probabilitiesDirichletthey must still sum to one after sampling

Using a normal distribution for a cost is a real error. It lets the model draw a negative cost.

They do not need to derive these. They need to recognise a wrong choice when supervising or reviewing, and to know that the choice is a stated methodological decision, not a software default to be left alone.

The result is a cloud, not a point

Plot every simulation on the cost-effectiveness plane. Each dot is one coherent version of the world.

How wide?

A tight cloud means the conclusion is robust. A sprawling one means you do not really know.

Which side of λ?

Count the dots below the threshold line. That proportion is your answer.

Which quadrants?

A cloud straddling the vertical axis means the intervention might not work at all.

The counting is done on net monetary benefit, not on the ratio.

Where net monetary benefit finally earns its keep. If they wondered why you spent a minute on it in 3B, this is the payoff.

The cloud, and the curve it generates

Same data as the tornado. Drag λ: the curve's height at any λ is just the share of the cloud below the dashed line.

Re-run to resample the cloud  ·  sph-aiimsbhopal.github.io/intro-economic-evaluation-health

The most important five minutes of this block. Make them drag λ slowly and watch the dashed line sweep the cloud while the dot climbs the curve. Then ask someone to state the relationship in their own words. When someone says "the curve is just the proportion of green dots at each threshold", the concept has landed and you can stop.

The acceptability curve

Threshold on the horizontal axis. Probability of being cost-effective on the vertical. One point for every value λ could take.

To build it: pick a λ, count the simulations with positive net monetary benefit, plot the proportion. Move λ. Repeat.

P(cost-effective at λ) = proportion of runs with (λ × ΔEi) − ΔCi > 0

It saves the decision-maker from committing to a threshold before seeing the answer.

Useful in India now, where the threshold is unsettled. You can say: below ₹1 lakh a poor bet, above ₹3 lakh a good one, in between it depends on a number not yet agreed.

That last sentence is the honest form of an Indian discussion section, and worth dictating. It uses the curve to sidestep a question the analyst has no authority to settle, which is exactly what the tool is for.

What a curve does not tell you

  • Not the probability that the intervention works. Only that it is good value, given the model
  • Not structural uncertainty. If the model is wrong, all ten thousand simulations are wrong together
  • Not whether you can afford it. That is budget impact, a separate calculation
  • Not a decision. A curve near 50% at every plausible threshold is a call for more research

And an 80% probability of being cost-effective still means a one-in-five chance you are wrong.

Say the last line slowly. Decision-makers routinely read 80% as "yes". It is not "yes", it is "probably", and that difference is what value-of-information analysis exists to price.

The best slide in the day. They saw the first two columns in Session 5 and concluded that the choice of trial moved the ICER by a third. Now show what it did to the probability: 4% against 57.3%, which is the difference between "no" and "maybe". Three things fall out of it: uncertainty is not decoration, the choice of effectiveness source is itself a decision, and a price negotiation can do more for value than any amount of further modelling. Give it two minutes.

Two more to know the names of

Scenario analysis

Not one parameter but a whole set of assumptions changed together: a different perspective, a shorter horizon, an explicit best and worst case. The Reference Case asks for these alongside probabilistic analysis.

Value of information

The formal answer to "should we do more research before deciding?" It prices the expected cost of getting the decision wrong, and so puts a value on reducing uncertainty. Beyond this session. See Drummond.

Naming the tool you did not use is better than pretending it does not exist.

Value of information deserves a session of its own and does not fit here. Naming it means they will recognise it in a paper and know what to look up.

11 Equity and 12 presentation

Equity

All lives and all QALYs count equally, whatever the age, sex or income. Use distributional analysis to show who gains, or extended analysis for financial risk protection.

Presentation

Report disaggregated and aggregated results. Give total costs and outcomes separately, not only the ratio. Justify any departure from the Reference Case.

In 2023 the Reference Case had to say no recognised threshold for India existed. Three years later, Chugh and colleagues published one.

You are learning this subject at the moment it is being settled.

End the technical content there. Land that last point properly. The guidance they are learning was written with an explicit placeholder, and that placeholder has just been filled. It is a live field, and they can contribute to it.

Recap

  • An ICER is a point estimate that inherits every input's uncertainty
  • Heterogeneity is not uncertainty. It gets subgroup analysis, planned in advance
  • One-way analysis shows what the answer turns on. It cannot give a probability
  • Probabilistic analysis gives every parameter a distribution. India asks for it in all analyses
  • The curve is just the cloud counted at every threshold, using net monetary benefit
