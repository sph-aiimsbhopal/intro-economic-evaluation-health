Economic Evaluation in Health  ·  Faculty workshop  ·  Session 1

Where economic evidence enters health policy

Who asks the questions in India, what they need from a study, and where faculty and residents come in.

School of Public Health, with the Regional Resource Centre for Health Technology Assessment, AIIMS Bhopal

Twenty minutes. The purpose is to answer "why am I here" for a room of clinicians, surgeons and residents from many departments, and to set up the paper each group is holding.

Today is about reading, not selling

By the end of the day you should be able to pick up an economic evaluation in your own field and say whether you believe it.

Your paper

Each group of three holds one published Indian evaluation. It stays with you all day.

Your checklist

After each session, fill in the rows it covered. Prompts are on your paper's sheet.

Your verdict

In the last session, in pairs of groups: would you act on this paper, and why?

Say plainly that this is not a course in writing business cases for equipment. The skill is appraisal: reading a published analysis the way you would read a trial, and knowing which choices decided its answer.

Four doors economic evidence walks through

DoorWhat gets decided
Health Technology Assessment in Indiawhether a programme, test or treatment should be adopted nationally or by a state
PM-JAY health benefit packageswhich procedures are covered, and at what package rate
State programmes and procurementwhat a state buys, at what scale, and in what order
Guidelines, journals, committeeswhat clinicians recommend, and which evidence is believed

Faculty sit at the fourth door every week, and increasingly at the other three.

The first three are formal. The fourth is informal and large: guideline panels, drug and therapeutics committees, peer review, the journal club where residents learn what counts as evidence. Ask who in the room has sat on any of these. Several will have.

Who asks the questions

  1. A programme, a ministry department or a state nominates a topic
  2. HTAIn prioritises it and assigns it to a Regional Resource Centre
  3. The Centre runs the study; its report is appraised, then endorsed
  4. The recommendation goes back to whoever asked, who decides

The question comes from the user. Anyone can suggest one.

Keep this to two minutes; Session 3 has the committees and the flow in detail. The point for now: HTAIn advises, it does not command. And topic selection is where much of the real influence lies: what nobody nominates, nobody evaluates.

These figures reappear in Session 3 when we discuss league tables; there you can move quickly. The message here is permission: they are allowed to disagree with a published paper, and should.

Where you come in

As a source of data

Models borrow costs, outcomes and quality-of-life weights. The best ones come from departments that measured them.

As a source of questions

You see which technologies are arriving, and which current practice is doubtful. That is a topic.

As an appraiser

Peer review, guideline panels, committees, journal club. Where a weak analysis should be caught.

As a co-investigator

With the Regional Resource Centre here, on studies that answer a user's question.

The brochure promised participants a route into producing HTA evidence. This slide is the first half of that promise; the close should give the concrete next step. Agree before the day what that next step is.
