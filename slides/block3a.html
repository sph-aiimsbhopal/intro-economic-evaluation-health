The Reference Case is organised on exactly the same principles as Drummond's Chapter 4. It is not an alternative framework, it is Chapter 4 with Indian defaults filled in. Tell them that, because it makes both documents easier to hold in the head at once.

Why it had to be written

A systematic review of Indian evaluations found analysts making different choices on the same questions:

PrincipleWhat Indian studies actually did
Perspectivepayer 48%, societal 38%, the rest unstated
Time horizonshort 38%, medium 27%, lifetime 17%
Outcome measureQALYs 29%, DALYs 9%, clinical outcomes 20%

Differences in method produce differences in ICER that have nothing to do with the interventions.

Reported in Sharma et al., 2023, from Prinja et al., Applied Health Economics and Health Policy, 2015.

Flag forward: Session 6 puts up India's league table, 79 studies ranked against each other. This slide is why that table cannot be read straight down the column. Tell them to remember it, and it is why standards came before more studies.

1 The decision problem

Most papers say "to assess the cost-effectiveness of X". That is a title, not a decision problem.

The Reference Case asks you to answer six questions:

who
does what
to whom
where
when
how often

Plus the policy question, the condition, the setting, the target population, and any subgroup whose costs or effects will differ. Identify those subgroups now, not after the results.

Make them do one out loud for the HPV programme. An ANM (who) gives a single dose of quadrivalent vaccine (does what) to 14-year-old girls (to whom) at government facilities with cold chain (where) from February 2026 (when) once (how often). Ninety seconds, and they will never write a vague aim again.

2 The comparator

"Any alternative will look good if it is compared to something that is sufficiently bad."

Indian Reference Case, on why comparator choice matters

The recommendation: compare against current practice in use. What the standard treatment guidelines say, or what is most widely used. Not the weakest option, and not "do nothing" unless doing nothing is genuinely what happens now.

Where a health benefit package is involved, the comparator is whatever the new intervention would replace inside it. Comparators need not be alike: a drug can be compared with surgery if they serve the same purpose.

The commonest flaw in the Indian literature and the easiest to spot. Give them the reading habit: find the comparator in the abstract before you read the ICER. If the comparator is "no screening" in a state that already runs opportunistic screening, the ICER is flattering and you know it in thirty seconds.

3 Perspective: whose costs count?

Cost itemHealthcare payerAbridged societal
Drugs, staff time, equipment, overheadsinin
Out-of-pocket payments by the patientoutin
Transport, food, lodging for patient and attendantoutin
Productivity lost to illness or early deathoutsensitivity only
Pain, fear, sufferingoutout

India's default is abridged societal, with the payer view reported alongside.

"Abridged" is doing real work in that phrase. Societal in that it includes what patients bear, abridged in that productivity losses stay out of the base case. Do not let them use the plain word "societal" in a paper or a review without saying which version they mean.

Why India insists on both

There is no single payer. Out-of-pocket spending is a large share of Indian health expenditure, and higher in the private sector, which treats most patients.

An analysis that ignores what families pay can call an intervention affordable while it is bankrupting the households using it.

Productivity losses stay out of the base case for three reasons: the costing methods disagree, Indian data barely exist, and including them favours diseases of the working and better-off over those of the poor, the young and the old.

That third reason is an equity argument inside a methods decision.

The third reason is the interesting one, and it is rarely noticed. Including productivity losses makes a working-age man's disease look more worth treating than an elderly widow's. That is a value judgement, and the Reference Case makes it deliberately rather than by accident. Ask whether they agree. There is a real argument on the other side.

4 Where the effectiveness estimate comes from

RankSource
1Systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised trials
2An existing systematic review, if relevant and of adequate quality
3Network meta-analysis, where no head-to-head trial exists
4Systematic review of non-randomised studies
5A single trial, then observational studies
lastExpert opinion, to be avoided

State the level of evidence you used. If you departed from the hierarchy, say why.

A de novo systematic review is often impossible inside a thesis timeline. Using a good existing one, appraised and cited, is explicitly allowed. Say so: it removes a common source of panic for the residents they supervise.

5 Costing, in three steps

Identify

Which resources does this intervention consume that the comparator does not? Staff, consumables, equipment, space, transport.

Measure

How much of each? Minutes of ANM time, doses, litres, square metres, visits.

Value

What is a unit worth? Salaries, procurement prices, package rates, rents. Prices are not costs, but they are what we have.

Getting step 1 wrong cannot be repaired by doing steps 2 and 3 beautifully.

Micro-costing is accurate and slow. Gross costing is fast and blunt.

A useful test for step 1: if both arms use the same resource in the same quantity, it cancels out of the incremental analysis and need not be costed at all. That insight saves weeks of pointless costing.

Capital costs, and the usual mistake

A cold-chain refrigerator lasts ten years. The programme you are evaluating uses one year of it.

Charge the programme an annuitised share, not the purchase price.

Spread the capital cost over the item's useful life at the discount rate, so a machine bought this year and one bought five years ago are treated alike. Buildings, vehicles, equipment and one-off training all belong here.

Omitting capital costs is the commonest costing error of all, and it always makes the intervention look cheaper.

If the room is comfortable, show the annuitisation factor on the board. If not, the concept is enough. They need to recognise the omission when reading, and to ask for it when supervising.

Your turn: which costs count?

Ten line items from a real programme costing. For each: is it direct or indirect, and is it counted under the payer view, the abridged societal view, or neither?

On your phone: sph-aiimsbhopal.github.io/intro-economic-evaluation-health

Six to eight minutes, in pairs. The items that generate argument are the out-of-pocket Pap smear, because they want to call anything patient-borne "indirect", and the lost day of wages, because they want it in the base case. Both arguments are worth having out loud before you resolve them.

7 Time horizon

Long enough for every cost and effect that differs.

SituationHorizon
Mortality or survival is affected: cancer, diabetes, cardiovascular diseaseLifetime
No difference in long-term sequelae: an acute infectionShort, and say why

Too short a horizon decides the result in advance.

Vaccinate a girl at 14 and the whole cost falls that year, while the cancers prevented would have come after 30. A one-year horizon counts every rupee and no benefit, so the programme looks like waste. The horizon produced that answer, not the vaccine.

Ask them what horizon they would defend for the HPV programme, and make them justify it. Lifetime, because mortality differs. Then point out that the same trap runs the other way: a horizon longer than the evidence supports is extrapolation, and it has to be declared and tested. They will meet both errors in print.

The point of this slide is that a well-framed clinical evaluation looks exactly like a well-framed programme evaluation. Two things to draw out. The perspective line is the abridged societal perspective in the wild, so they can recognise the phrase in a methods section. And row 4 is unusual and admirable: rather than pick a trial, the authors carried both through and reported both answers. Ask the groups whether their own paper names its effectiveness source at all.

Recap

  • The decision problem is six questions, not a title
  • The comparator is current practice. A weak one flatters everything downstream
  • India's default is abridged societal, payer view alongside
  • Costs: identify, measure, value. Annuitise capital
  • Check the National Health System Cost Database first
  • The horizon must be long enough for benefits to arrive

All of that is the numerator, on paper. Next: the numerator, on your own ward.

That last line is the bridge. They have just been told how costing should be done; Session 4 makes them do it for a service they actually run, which is where the principles stop being abstract. The denominator comes in Session 5.
