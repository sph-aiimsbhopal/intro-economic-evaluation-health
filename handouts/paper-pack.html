Group appraisal exercise

Your group has one paper, and it stays with you all day

Each group of three has been given one published economic evaluation from India. After each session, spend a few minutes applying what was just taught to your paper, using the prompts on its sheet and the appraisal checklist. By the report-back at 15:55 the checklist should be full and you should be able to say, in one sentence, whether you believe the paper's conclusion.

The eight papers were chosen to differ. Some are strong, some are not, and at least one calls itself something it is not. Do not assume that publication means quality, or that a paper from a well-known group is right.

Group 1

Oncology and surgery
reports with Group 2

Sentinel lymph-node biopsy guided neck dissection versus elective neck dissection in the management of early-stage oral cancer: a cost-utility analysis

Thiagarajan S, Sharda S, Chugh Y, Gupta N, Pramesh CS, Prinja S. Cancer Med 2026;15(2):e71571. PMID 41644818  ·  PMC12875840  ·  doi:10.1002/cam4.71571

Open accessFull text free at doi.org/10.1002/cam4.71571. Read it; this sheet only gets you started.

What the paper says it did, and found

A Markov model follows a hypothetical cohort of patients in India with early oral squamous cell carcinoma, tracking disease-free survival, recurrence and overall survival. Three strategies are compared: sentinel lymph-node biopsy (SLNB) guided neck dissection; elective neck dissection (END) alone; and END with frozen section.

Payer perspective, lifetime horizon, costs and QALYs discounted at 3%. One-way and probabilistic sensitivity analyses.

SLNB costs INR 5,564 more per patient than END alone and INR 2,507 more than END with frozen section, and gains 0.31 and 0.33 QALYs respectively. The reported incremental cost-utility ratios are INR 8,088 and INR 16,709 per QALY. Judged against one times GDP per capita, SLNB has a 94% probability of being cost-effective. The authors support its inclusion in the government insurance scheme.

Review for cost-effectiveness analysis of laparoscopic Intra-peritoneal Onlay Mesh for ventral hernia repair in Indian settings

Guleria C, Kumar D, Sahoo KC. Cost Eff Resour Alloc 2025;23(1):27. PMID 40495189  ·  doi:10.1186/s12962-025-00638-4

Open accessFull text free at doi.org/10.1186/s12962-025-00638-4. Read it; this sheet only gets you started.

What the paper says it did, and found

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 10 randomised trials (1,204 patients) comparing laparoscopic intra-peritoneal onlay mesh (IPOM) repair with open ventral hernia repair, combined with a cost-effectiveness analysis from the Indian health system perspective over five years. Costs come from the National Health System Cost Database.

Hernia recurrence, the primary outcome, did not differ (risk ratio 1.28, 95% CI 0.81 to 2.04). Wound infection was lower with the laparoscopic repair (risk ratio 0.31, 0.18 to 0.54).

The ICER is INR 5,023 per wound infection averted, set against a threshold of INR 2,14,000 based on India's per-capita income. In a probabilistic analysis of 10,000 simulations the laparoscopic repair was cost-effective in 54.0%. The authors conclude it is not clinically effective for recurrence and less likely to be cost-effective.

Cost-utility analysis of heart surgeries for young adults with severe rheumatic mitral valve disease in India

Uy J, Ketkar AG, Portnoy A, Kim JJ. Int J Cardiol 2021;338:50-57. PMID 34090957  ·  doi:10.1016/j.ijcard.2021.05.059

Not open accessWork from this sheet and the abstract. Not reported in the abstract is an acceptable answer to a prompt, and worth saying out loud.

What the paper says it did, and found

A Markov model of a hypothetical cohort of 20-year-olds in India with severe rheumatic mitral valve disease, from an Indian public payer perspective, estimating lifetime costs and QALYs.

Three strategies: valve repair; mechanical valve replacement; bioprosthetic valve replacement. The comparator is a standard-of-care mix approximating Indian practice: 32% repair, 33% mechanical, 35% bioprosthetic.

Results per patient (US dollars): repair $2,530, 9.7 QALYs; standard of care $2,990, 8.7 QALYs; mechanical $3,220, 6.2 QALYs; bioprosthetic $3,190, 10.1 QALYs.

Repair is cheaper and more effective than both standard of care and mechanical replacement. Bioprosthetic replacement against repair costs $1,590 per QALY, which the authors say may be cost-effective against India's GDP per capita ($2,005). They conclude that repair is optimal, mechanical replacement should not be recommended for this group, and bioprosthetic replacement may be cost-effective where repair quality is not assured, newer valves are used, or valve prices fall.

Cost-effectiveness of population-based screening for diabetes and hypertension in India: an economic modelling study

Kaur G, Chauhan AS, Prinja S, Teerawattananon Y, Muniyandi M, Rastogi A, Jyani G, Nagarajan K, Lakshmi P, Gupta A, Selvam JM, Bhansali A, Jain S. Lancet Public Health 2022;7(1):e65-e73. PMID 34774219  ·  doi:10.1016/S2468-2667(21)00199-7

Open accessFull text free at doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(21)00199-7. Read it; this sheet only gets you started.

What the paper says it did, and found

A hybrid decision tree and Markov model of a cohort aged 30, over a lifetime, from a societal perspective that includes health-system and out-of-pocket costs, discounted at 3%. Population-based screening at different ages, frequencies and tests is compared with current practice, where diabetes and hypertension are detected opportunistically.

Costs come from primary data in Haryana and Tamil Nadu, the National Health System Cost Database, the Costing of Health Services in India study, national survey data and insurance package rates. Quality of life was measured with EQ-5D-5L in 962 patients. Funded by the Department of Health Research.

Judged against one times GDP per capita, no screening strategy was cost-effective at current levels of health-care use; even screening once in 20 years had only a 60% probability. But if 20% of newly diagnosed patients were treated through Health and Wellness Centres, screening people aged 30 to 65 every three or five years became cost-effective, and at 70% annual screening became cost saving.

To compare cost effectiveness of 'Kangaroo Ward Care' with 'Intermediate intensive care' in stable very low birth weight infants (birth weight < 1100 grams): a randomized control trial

Sharma D, Murki S, Oleti TP. Ital J Pediatr 2016;42:64. PMID 27412638  ·  doi:10.1186/s13052-016-0274-3

Open accessFull text free at doi.org/10.1186/s13052-016-0274-3. Read it; this sheet only gets you started.

What the paper says it did, and found

A randomised trial in a tertiary neonatal unit in Hyderabad. 141 stable very low birth weight infants (71 and 70) were randomised to Kangaroo Ward Care, moving to the kangaroo ward with the mother at 1,150 g, or Intermediate Intensive Care in an incubator or warmer until 1,250 g.

Hospital costs were estimated top down and parents' out-of-pocket costs bottom up, from randomisation to discharge.

Mean cost was INR 41,592 per infant with Kangaroo Ward Care and INR 75,389 with intermediate care, a saving of about INR 33,800 (US$512) per infant. NICU and hospital stays were shorter. The authors conclude that early transfer to the kangaroo ward is a cost-effective intervention.

Cost-effectiveness of noninvasive ventilation for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease-related respiratory failure in Indian hospitals without ICU facilities

Patel SP, Pena ME, Babcock CI. Lung India 2015;32(6):549-556. PMID 26664158  ·  PMC4663855  ·  doi:10.4103/0970-2113.168137

Open accessFull text free at doi.org/10.4103/0970-2113.168137. Read it; this sheet only gets you started.

What the paper says it did, and found

A decision-analytic model comparing ward-based non-invasive ventilation (NIV) with standard treatment against standard treatment alone for COPD-related respiratory failure in Indian hospitals that have no ICU.

All parameters come from the published literature. Societal perspective, lifetime horizon, future costs discounted at 3%, costs in 2012 US dollars. One-way, two-way and probabilistic sensitivity analyses.

NIV gave 17.7% more survival, cost $101 more and gained 1.67 QALYs. The paper reports cost per QALY of $78 for standard care ($535.02 ÷ 6.82) and $75 for NIV ($636.33 ÷ 8.49), and an ICER of $61 per QALY, far below India's GDP per capita ($1,489). NIV was preferred in 100% of simulations at a willingness to pay above $250.

Peritoneal dialysis-first initiative in India: a cost-effectiveness analysis

Gupta D, Jyani G, Ramachandran R, Bahuguna P, Ameel M, Dahiya BB, Kohli HS, Prinja S, Jha V. Clin Kidney J 2022;15(1):128-135. PMID 35035943  ·  PMC8757426  ·  doi:10.1093/ckj/sfab126

Open accessFull text free at doi.org/10.1093/ckj/sfab126. Read it; this sheet only gets you started.

What the paper says it did, and found

A Markov model with one-year cycles compares starting kidney replacement therapy with peritoneal dialysis (PD first) against starting with haemodialysis (HD first) in people with kidney failure in India, over their lifetime, with complications such as peritonitis, vascular access problems and blood-borne infections.

Two scenarios: a real-world one, using current costs and patterns of use; and a public programme one, following the Pradhan Mantri National Dialysis Programme. Health system costs, patients' out-of-pocket spending and indirect costs are all included; 3% discounting.

Mean QALYs were 3.3 with PD and 1.6 with HD. From a societal perspective PD first is cost-saving in both scenarios. If only costs directly attributable to care are counted, PD first is cost-effective only if PD consumables cost INR 70 per unit, 65% below the recommended price of INR 200. The authors conclude the government should negotiate the price of PD consumables under the programme.

reports with Group 7

Cost-effectiveness of treating childhood acute myeloid leukemia at a tertiary care center in North India

Srinivasan S, Bolous NS, Batra A, Bharti S, Singh N, Shaikh T, Yadav A, Kanwar V. Pediatr Blood Cancer 2024;71(11):e31242. PMID 39126354  ·  doi:10.1002/pbc.31242

Open accessFull text free at doi.org/10.1002/pbc.31242. Read it; this sheet only gets you started.

What the paper says it did, and found

A retrospective study of children under 15 with acute myeloid leukaemia treated at one tertiary cancer centre in North India. Outcomes, complications, deaths and costs were taken from the electronic medical record and the hospital database.

Of 59 children, 43 received intensive therapy. Treatment-related mortality reached 30%, mostly from sepsis; abandonment and limited access to bone marrow transplantation were also problems. Three-year event-free survival was 24.5% and overall survival 27.9% in those treated intensively.

The total cost per newly diagnosed child treated with curative intent was US$4,454. Cost-effectiveness was measured as cost per DALY averted, which came to 24% of GDP per capita; the authors judge treatment cost-effective even against a stringent threshold, and call for reducing treatment-related deaths.

A published Comment on this paper appeared in the same journal (Sra, Bakhshi, Ganguly, Pediatr Blood Cancer 2025;72(1):e31305, PMID 39228042).
